# Five photographed colours anchor an unmeasured screen

Almost nothing about the DMG-01 screen's optics has ever been measured. The only calibrated physical data are **BGB's five photographed colours** (four shades plus the LCD-disabled colour). The only hard electrical facts are a traced schematic, a Nintendo patent and a few multimeter readings on single units. Everything finer is unpublished: twist, cell gap, mixture, polarizer angles, glass and lens thickness, dot gap, response time and drive levels. The physics in Retro Display Lab (RDL), and so in Vector LCD, is a reconstruction from period literature. The second research round found the best period match yet for that reconstruction: a 1988 Citizen paper with a yellow-mode STN recipe of Δn·d 950 nm, d/p 0.7, 4–7 µm cell gap and a ZLI-2293 base. Its measured response times bracket the simulator's 150 ms. The round also confirmed the driver chips from Nintendo's own patent and showed that the LCD board has no temperature sensor. It corrected several first-round claims. The "green colour-filter film" repair was a Game Boy Pocket. The -11 V/-14 V wheel reading came from a faulty board. BGB's navy high-contrast image is BGB's own simulation, not a photo. Taken together, the simulator's main values are consistent with the evidence. A few of its labels claim more than the sources support, and some cheap refinements are available (the first blank frame, the dark line at power-off, reflector grain). One finding needs the owner's attention: BGB's measured LCD-disabled colour is slightly *darker* than shade 0, not lighter.

## Construction: the stack is known in outline, never in numbers

**What is documented.** Nintendo's own technical data gives an **STN dot-matrix LCD, 47 × 43 mm, 160 × 144 dots** ([Nintendo technical data](https://www.nintendo.com/fr-fr/Assistance/Consoles-plus-anciennes/Donnees-techniques-619585.html)). That works out to a dot pitch of about **0.294 × 0.299 mm** and a diagonal of 2.51". The "2.4/2.5/2.6 inch" figures quoted elsewhere are loose. The layer order is the usual reflective sandwich: front polarizer, two glass sheets with the crystal between them, rear polarizer, reflector ([Hackaday 2018](https://hackaday.com/2018/01/26/repairing-a-sunburned-game-boy-screen/)). First-hand repair accounts fill in detail. The DMG front polarizer is "really glued down" to the glass with clear adhesive ([Wompratz video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q--apvHPYLY)). On the back, the **reflector and rear polarizer are two separate films**, peeled off one at a time ([optroodt](https://optroodt.github.io/blog/game-boy-backlight-mod)). One backlight guide says the rear polarizer looks "greenish" when peeled, against light grey once it is removed ([Instructables](https://instructables.com/How-to-Backlight-a-Game-Boy-DMG)). That hints that part of the tint may sit in the film itself, but it is an unexplained anecdote. A correction to the first round: the Hackaday "sunburned" repair, whose comment section mentioned a "colour filter film that removes the green tint", is a **Game Boy Pocket**, as other commenters point out. It says nothing about extra films in a DMG.

**Maker and panel type.** Sharp made the driver chips (below). The common account says Sharp supplied the screen after Nintendo rejected a TN proposal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy)), but no primary document names the glass maker or a panel part number. "Sharp made the panel" is likely, not proven. The panel's mode is never named either. The yellow-green resting colour, the blue-black dark shades, and the fact that turning the front polarizer 90° inverts the image and turns it blue ([gekkio bivert review](https://gekkio.fi/blog/2020/hhl-bivert-v2-technical-review/)) all fit a **single-cell "yellow mode" STN**. That is an inference.

**The best period match for the crystal recipe.** No DMG twist, gap, mixture or polarizer angle exists in any language. The second round found the likely origin of RDL's numbers. In 1988 Citizen Watch published a fast **270° left-handed yellow-mode STN**: SiO oblique-evaporated alignment, **cell gap 4–7 µm, Δn·d = 950 nm, d/p = 0.7, ZLI-2293-based mixtures**, Arisawa LN-1205T polarizers with absorption axes at about ±50° to the horizontal, tested at 1/100 and 1/200 duty at 20 °C ([Okada et al., ITE Tech. Rep. 12(6), 1988](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/tvtr/12/6/12_KJ00001957967/_pdf)). Its measured threshold is about **1.3–1.65 V**, and its curve switches between about **1.7 and 1.9 V rms** at 4 µm. A single pass lets through about **33–35 % in the light state and 2 % in the dark state**. This is a period panel from another maker. It shows what Japanese STN practice looked like in 1988; it is not the DMG.

**Lens, air gap and glass.** A modding book says only that the LCD sits behind "a **high-impact plastic** lens", fitted into a recess of the front shell "like a sticker" ([No Starch, *Game Boy Modding* ch. 2](https://nostarch.com/download/samples/GameBoyModding_chapter2.pdf)). "Acrylic" appears only in a seller's guide. A reproduction plastic lens is sold as "1.0 mm thick" and said to "fit nicely" ([Hand Held Legend](https://handheldlegend.com/products/game-boy-dmg-cover-screen)). Buyers say thicker glass lenses do not sit flush ([ZedLabz](https://zedlabz.com/en-ax/collections/retro-nintendo-game-boy-series/products/glass-screen-lens-for-game-boy-dmg-01-zero-projects-gbz-cover-replacement-black-zedlabz)), so the original is probably about 1 mm or slightly less. A loose replacement polarizer can be laid "into the front half of the shell" under the LCD ([Hand Held Legend film](https://handheldlegend.com/products/polarization-film)), which proves an air gap exists but not its size. **No caliper value exists** for the original lens, the air gap, the glass, any film, or the module. The only Game Boy film thickness on record, 0.28 mm, is for a Game Boy Light transflective film ([ZedLabz](https://www.zedlabz.com/products/transflective-polarising-film-for-game-boy-light-self-adhesive-polarizer-polarizing-70-x-67mm-2-pack-3dlens)).

**Dots and reflector texture.** No scaled micrograph of DMG dots was found. BGB's close-up drawing, with a dot fill of 0.875 and a gap in the LCD-disabled colour, is a guess by its author's own statement: "I don't have a way to actually zoom in like this … so i don't know how wide the edges actually are" ([BGB reality](https://bgb.bircd.org/reality/)). One new data point comes from the Brick Boy developer. Measured from close-up photos of LCD modules, the reflector has a **paper-like grain of 0.3–0.5 dot (about 0.1–0.15 mm), with no direction, varying reflectance by ±3.9 %**. It shows in light areas and disappears in dark ones ([Time Extension, 2026](https://www.timeextension.com/news/2026/08/the-most-gorgeous-game-boy-emulation-ive-ever-seen-brick-boy-goes-the-extra-mile-to-copy-the-consoles-nostalgic-display)). This is the developer's own image measurement; the unit and photos are not identified.

## Drive electronics: a traced board, two named chips, no voltages for the drive levels

**Chips.** Nintendo's US patent for the Game Boy names the drivers outright: the common (row) driver "may, for example, be a **Sharp LH 5076 F**" and the segment (column) driver "a **Sharp LH 5077 F**", on a "144×160 matrix" ([US5184830A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5184830A/en)). That independently confirms Gekkio's trace of the later DMG-LCD-06 board, and it contradicts the gbdev wiki's "LH5028" listing. Gekkio's schematic shows an **IR3E02 "STN LCD bias generation" chip** that makes the five drive levels V1–V5. Each level is smoothed by a **1 µF capacitor (C6–C10)**. Beside it sit a **30 kΩ contrast pot (VR1)** feeding the chip's VR input and a 2SA933 transistor, probably a buffer. **The board has no thermistor or other temperature-sensing part** ([Gekkio DMG-LCD-06](https://github.com/Gekkio/gb-schematics/blob/main/DMG-LCD-06/schematic/DMG-LCD-06.pdf)). The patent calls the wheel a "brightness (contrast) volume" feeding an "LCD buffer amplifier" ([JPH02191484A](https://patents.google.com/patent/JPH02191484A/en)). In plain terms, the wheel sets a reference voltage; it is not a resistor in the drive path. No datasheet for the LH5076, LH5077 or IR3E02 exists in any archive searched, including every Sharp data book on bitsavers.

**Supply and the wheel's range.** The negative supply is "**nominal -18 V (unregulated)**" on every DC/DC board revision. Only the +5 V output is regulated ([Gekkio DMG-CPU-06](https://github.com/Gekkio/gb-schematics/blob/main/DMG-CPU-06/schematic/DMG-CPU-06.pdf); [DC-CONV-DMG-AB](https://github.com/Gekkio/gb-schematics/blob/main/DC-CONV-DMG-AB/schematic/DC-CONV-DMG-AB.pdf)). The "-19 V" in forum posts is a recollection ("-19v I think it was"). Read in full, the wheel-voltage thread is weaker than its summaries suggested. The **-11 V (minimum) to -14 V (maximum)** readings were taken with an analog meter on a **faulty** DMG ("stuck on max contrast"), at an unstated pin. The "**-13 V for good contrast**" figure is a single reading from a different person's working unit ([chipmusic.org topic 15931](https://chipmusic.org/forums/topic/15931/dmg-screen-stuck-on-max-contrast/)). Ratios of those numbers (-11/-13 ≈ 0.85, -14/-13 ≈ 1.08) match the simulator's wheel, but the match depends on the reference point. Counted from the +5 V top of the ladder rather than from ground, the total span is 1.19 instead of 1.27. The pot voltage is also a reference fed into the bias chip, not the voltage across a dot. The same thread gives two capacitor readings at full clockwise wheel (-12.5 V and -9.9 V on a good board). The other three appeared only in a photo that is no longer available. A 2009 thread found desoldered "30k" pots reading about 17 kΩ, which remains unexplained ([gbdev forum topic 55](https://gbdev.gg8.se/forums/viewtopic.php?id=55)).

**How the levels probably work.** A sibling Sharp driver from the 1986 data book, the LH5021A, is rated for a **-17 V typical LCD supply (absolute maximum -20 V)** and **-20 to +70 °C**. A polarity signal swaps each output between pairs of levels, which is the standard six-level scheme ([Sharp 1986 MOS Data Book, pp. 870–876](https://bitsavers.org/components/sharp/_dataBooks/1986_Sharp_MOS_Semiconductor_Data_Book.pdf)). The DMG wiring fits the same scheme: rows on V1/V4/V5, columns on V2/V3/V5, both with the FR polarity signal. Period practice set the select/non-select voltage ratio at its theoretical optimum. Citizen used 1.106 at 1/100 duty and 1.073 at 1/200, exactly the optimum formula ([Okada 1988](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/tvtr/12/6/12_KJ00001957967/_pdf)). For the DMG's 144 rows the same rule gives a ratio of **1.087 (1/13 bias)**. That is an inference about what Sharp would have done, not a measurement.

**Timing.** The screen refreshes at **59.7275 Hz**: 154 line periods of about 108.7 µs each, 144 of them visible ([mGBA gbdoc](https://github.com/mgba-emu/gbdoc/blob/gh-pages/index.md); [Pan Docs](https://gbdev.io/pandocs/Rendering.html)). A whole row is latched at once ([Spurden](https://thomas.spurden.name/blog/capturing-gb-lcd/)). Each row is therefore selected for 1 of 154 line times, slightly less than 1/144. How the four shades are made from two data bits per pixel is unknown. RDL infers pulse-width steps within the line from four clock pulses per line. **The FR polarity period has never been captured**, and no oscilloscope trace of the drive levels exists.

**LCD disabled.** Pan Docs: "the screen is blank, which on DMG is displayed as a white 'whiter' than color #0". Also, "when re-enabling the LCD … the screen will stay blank during the first frame" ([Pan Docs LCDC](https://gbdev.io/pandocs/LCDC.html)). Nintendo's 1999 manual warns that on "early DMGs, a black horizontal line appears" if the LCD is stopped outside vertical blank ([gbdev forum pid 967](https://gbdev.gg8.se/forums/viewtopic.php?pid=967)).

## Colours: one calibrated photo set, and an unexpected brightness order

**BGB is the only calibrated source, and only for five patches.** BGB lit "gameboys" with colour-accurate light and corrected the photos with a ColorChecker "to true colors and brightness in sRGB gamma 2.2". It sampled each patch defocused at 1/60 s, avoided reflections and averaged several photos ([BGB reality](https://bgb.bircd.org/reality/index.html)). Pixel values read from the published image are **LCD disabled #948A04, shades #759833, #588F51, #3B7560, #2E615A** ([palette image](https://bgb.bircd.org/reality/dmg-reality-colorscheme.png)). The page **does not say** how many units were photographed, at what wheel position, whether through the lens, at what light angle or brightness. No spectrophotometer, colorimeter or CIE data for any DMG was found in English, Japanese or Chinese. Every other palette is chosen by eye or of unknown origin: SameBoy, Gambatte, the "pea soup" set, Virtual Console ([SameBoy display.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/LIJI32/SameBoy/master/Core/display.c); [pixel-editor](https://www.pixel-editor.com/palettes/gameboy-green)). Because the photos were defocused, each shade is an average of dot and gap. BGB notes that the gap is yellow and the dot itself therefore "more bluish" than its palette entry.

**Only the five patches are measurements.** The rest of BGB's colour work is software design. Its wheel illustration runs from all-yellow at the bottom to **deep navy (#00005E) at the top** ([contrastwheel.png](https://bgb.bircd.org/reality/contrastwheel.png)). That image is BGB's simulation, not a photograph, so it does not undermine v0.32.1's withdrawal of navy. BGB's wheel sentence, "As in reality, the lightest shade is the 'LCD disabled' color, and darkest shade if the contrast is darker than normal, is darker than the darkest shade", is an author's observation without numbers. BGB's flicker gradient puts the LCD-disabled colour at the light end, beyond shade 0. A lightest/darkest flicker lands on **#4A8259**, not the plain average #588047 ([dmgblend-shades.png](https://bgb.bircd.org/reality/dmgblend-shades.png)).

**A brightness question to flag.** Computed with standard sRGB luminance (my arithmetic), BGB's **LCD-disabled colour (luminance 0.245, L* 56.6) is slightly darker than shade 0 (0.265, L* 58.5)**. It is far yellower, but not lighter. Pan Docs calls it "whiter" than colour 0, and BGB places it at the light end. BGB's Pocket palette behaves as expected: disabled 0.589 against shade 0 at 0.482. So for the DMG, "whiter" must mean less green or less blue, not brighter, or the measurement has a small error. Either way, a simulator rule that maps states "lighter than white" toward #948A04 makes the colour slightly *darker* as the crystal state gets *lighter*. That is worth knowing, not necessarily wrong. The measured range from shade 0 to shade 3 is only **2.7 : 1 in luminance**, far below the roughly 17 : 1 of Citizen's single-pass cell. Yellow gaps, glare and the diffuse reflector plausibly explain the difference, but no source quantifies it.

**Variation, the lens and ageing.** No data quantifies differences between units or production years, or colour drift with age. Sources say only that colour depends on lighting, the wheel, battery and ageing ([NESdev](https://nesdev.nes.science/f20/t12533.xhtml)). Documented ageing is polarizer and adhesive "sunburn", plus "screen rot" specks. Whether BGB shot through the lens cannot be settled. "Avoiding reflections" fits a glossy lens, but the front polarizer is glossy too. Off-axis colour and colour at known wheel positions are undocumented for the DMG.

## Behaviour: qualitative facts, period numbers, almost no DMG measurements

**Response and ghosting.** No photodiode or high-speed-camera measurement of a DMG exists. The DMG-specific facts are qualitative. A two-frame flicker fuses into a steady in-between shade, which games exploited ([Hackaday 2023](https://hackaday.com/2023/10/27/why-game-boy-ips-screens-flicker)). The in-between shade lies on the real colour path. The DMG is slower than the Pocket ("a bit faster than the DMG LCD", [BGB](https://bgb.bircd.org/reality/index.html)). BGB's blend coefficients and test ROMs are unpublished. The period numbers come from Citizen's Fig. 10, reporting the average of switch-on and switch-off times (read from the graph, approximate). With a ZLI-2293/RO-TN-403 mixture: **about 150 ms at 4 µm, 210 ms at 5 µm, 260 ms at 6 µm, 330 ms at 7 µm**. With the lower-viscosity mixture: 65, 95, 165 and 290 ms. Response grows with the number of rows ([Okada 1988](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/tvtr/12/6/12_KJ00001957967/_pdf)). Vendors quote STN at about 250 ms at 25 °C, much slower when cold ([Orient Display](https://orientdisplay.com/?p=308463)). The rise and fall times of a DMG specifically are unknown.

**Contrast wheel.** No photo or video series of a healthy DMG across the wheel was obtainable (YouTube and Reddit refused automated access). That leaves BGB's sentence and the owner's own video frames. The slow colour changes in forum threads ("2–3 seconds", "about 90 seconds") were on faulty boards and say nothing about the crystal. A degraded polarizer's blotches show "when you go dark" ([Wompratz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q--apvHPYLY)). No source reports hysteresis or lag when turning the wheel.

**Temperature.** No DMG data. Because no hardware compensation exists, the user corrects by hand with the wheel. A general article says the screen looks faint on a cold morning ([Game Boy Museum](https://www.gameboymuseum.com/single-post/game-boy-hardware-explained-part-3-the-screen)). Heating the ribbon makes the screen "discolor … but it will come back to normal after a minute or two" ([iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/Answers/View/26113/Blank+Screen+when+turned+on)). LCDs in general go black around 60 °C and recover on cooling ([Citizen FAQ](https://citizenwatch-global.com/faq/detail/9)). The DMG's clearing temperature is unknown.

**Burn-in, power-off and lines.** No report of a DMG keeping a ghost of a long static picture was found. The documented case is different: a stopped scan. A game crash froze "the scanline beam in place for, maybe 10 minutes". It left a dark horizontal line that faded partly "in the first 4 minutes", then slowly over a day, and stayed visible at certain angles. kevtris attributed it to DC across the cell ("LCDs don't like DC on them … if it doesn't go away after a few hours of use it most likely is permanent", [daid/dmg-lcd-damage](https://github.com/daid/dmg-lcd-damage)). At an ordinary power-off or a badly timed LCD-off, "a black line appears for a second and then fades" ([NESdev archive](https://nesdev.nes.science/f20/t12568.xhtml)). Pan Docs describes the LCD-off line as "similar to that which appears when the GB is turned off". One chipmusic post speaks of a "bright" line, but the primary pages consistently say black. How the rest of the picture fades at power-off has never been described or timed. The general mechanism behind ion sticking was measured in 1994 by Merck Japan on STN test cells ([Nakazono 1994](https://doi.org/10.11538/ekitouyokou.20.0_374)), not on a DMG.

**Streaking and viewing angle.** No source shows crosstalk streaks on a healthy DMG. Vertical and horizontal line faults are ribbon-bond failures ([Retrofixes](https://www.retrofixes.com/2013/07/fix-vertical-lines-in-gameboy-screens.html); [blog.gg8.se](https://blog.gg8.se/wordpress/2011/07/21/why-dead-horizontal-lines-are-harder-to-fix/)). No source describes how a stock DMG changes when tilted. The only angle data are Citizen's T-V curves at 0°, 20° and 40° for its own cell.

## What only a real DMG can settle

The gaps cluster into three groups, and a few cheap measurements would close most of them. **Electrical:** V1–V5 at both wheel ends and at normal (a multimeter on C6–C10), the FR polarity period, and the drive waveforms on an oscilloscope. These would replace the inferred 1/13 bias and the anecdotal wheel range with numbers. **Mechanical:** calipers on the original lens, the lens-to-LCD gap, the glass and the films, and the lens material. These set the reflector and bezel shadow sizes and the glare. **Optical and temporal:** a calibrated photo series across the wheel, colour with and without the lens, a photodiode or 240 fps video of a shade change at known temperature, the dot gap under a microscope, the power-off fade, and colour at an angle. Unresolved points: the LCD-disabled colour's luminance being below shade 0, the 17 kΩ reading of "30 kΩ" pots, the "greenish" rear polarizer, the LH5076/77 and IR3E02 datasheets, the Japanese priority filing behind the US patent, and the 1986 Nintendo "filter on the LCD board" patent (JPS6241682A). Reddit and YouTube restoration videos, the likeliest sources of caliper posts and wheel sweeps, could not be read from this session.

## Conclusion

The research shifts where the simulator's authority comes from. Its crystal physics now has a named period ancestor (Citizen 1988) rather than only an open-source reconstruction, and its driver chips have Nintendo's own patent behind them. But its two most "measured" inputs, the wheel range and the colour path at the extremes, rest on thinner evidence than the current labels say: a faulty board read with a needle meter, and a single sentence from an emulator author. The honest framing for the Info tab is "period-typical physics, calibrated to five photographed colours".

Two things deserve a decision before the next visible change. First, BGB's LCD-disabled colour is measurably darker than its "white", so the light end of the colour path is not monotonic in brightness. Either accept it as BGB's measurement, or flag that Pan Docs' "whiter" suggests a small error in it. Second, three low-risk, evidence-backed additions are available: a blank first frame after re-enable, an optional one-second dark-row flash at LCD-off, and the static reflector grain. Each can be labelled from its anecdotal or photo-derived source without inventing anything.
